Week 03 / 15 October 2026

Web scraping with BBC iPlayer

Learning two scraping methods and trying Python with BBC iPlayer.

Method: Python / Google ColabSource: BBC iPlayer homepage

What we learned this week

This week, we learned two ways to collect information from webpages. Chris used BBC iPlayer to show how programme titles and descriptions can be saved as a dataset. He also explained how the size and position of programme cards can make some programmes easier to notice.

Web Scraper extension: We learned how to create a sitemap in Chrome, add selectors and preview the selected content. The tool can first find each programme card, then find the title inside it. The results can be exported as a CSV file.

Python and Google Colab: We also learned how to run a Python script in a notebook. The example uses Requests to get the webpage and Beautiful Soup to find information in its HTML. It collects titles and descriptions, which can then be saved as a text or CSV file.

What I tried

I used the workshop’s Google Colab notebook to collect titles and descriptions from BBC iPlayer. I ran the code and checked the results. It collected 106 records, including some repeated programmes. I then opened the data in a spreadsheet.

Source: BBC iPlayer

What I learned

Both methods use the webpage’s structure to find information. We used Inspect in Chrome to look at HTML tags and class names. I learned to preview the results and check whether they match what I wanted. I also practised running the Python example in Colab and opening the results in a spreadsheet.

Week 04 / 22 October 2026

Collecting data about university AI policies

Choosing a topic and turning written guidance into categories.

Method: Manual data collectionSources: Official university guidance

What we did this week

Our group chose the data analysis company scenario. We wanted to explore how UK universities explain acceptable and unacceptable uses of generative AI in assessments. We first discussed our topic, ethical questions and possible gaps in the data, before deciding where to look.

We chose five universities: Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial College London, UCL and LSE. Our table includes AI brainstorming, AI grammar checking, AI-generated content, policy level and source links.

My contribution

I was responsible for Cambridge and Imperial College London. I looked for official guidance and helped classify the information. We used numerical codes for permitted, conditional, prohibited and unclear uses.

Some information was easier to find in department guidance than in general university guidance. For example, Cambridge’s Faculty of Classics discusses grammar suggestions. One Imperial source was an older Earth Science and Engineering handbook. I needed to note the department and year rather than treat these as rules for everyone.

What I learned

It was not always easy to put a policy into one category. A rule could depend on the department or the assignment. I learned that “unclear” does not mean “prohibited”, and that source links and short notes help explain our choices.

Our table describes the guidance we found. It does not show how students actually use AI or whether the rules work well.

Next step

We will check our categories together and use the group dataset in the data visualisation workshop.

Week 05 / 29 October 2026

Exploring data visualisation

Thinking about what data can show and how to present it clearly.

Topic: Data visualisationTools introduced: Excel / Tableau Public

What we learned this week

We discussed the data we collected last week and the problems we found. Our group looked at AI policies from five UK universities. We found that the wording of some policies was unclear, which made it difficult to compare them.

We also learned about data visualisation. Holly showed us how charts can make information easier to understand. We looked at examples such as bar charts, pie charts and heatmaps, and learned that different charts are useful for different questions.

Workshop practice

In the workshop, we explored ways to make charts using Excel. We also discussed who a visualisation is for and what we want people to understand from it. I am still learning how to choose a suitable chart for different types of data.

What I learned

I learned that making a chart is not just about choosing colours or making something look nice. We need to check our data first and think about what it can and cannot tell us. For our AI policy topic, an unclear policy should not be treated as a rule that prohibits AI.

I would like to practise using Excel more and try a simple visualisation of our group data.
